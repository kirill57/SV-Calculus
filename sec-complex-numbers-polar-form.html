    Section 14.8 Complex numbers and polar form

    Polar coordinates describe a point by distance and angle. Complex numbers give another way to do the same thing.
    🔗
    At first this looks like notation. Then multiplication reveals the surprise.
    🔗
    In ordinary coordinates, multiplying two points does not make sense. In complex coordinates, multiplying two points means:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{multiply the distances and add the angles.} \end{equation*}
    That is why complex numbers are so useful for rotation and oscillation.
    🔗

    Subsection Complex numbers as points in the plane

    A complex number has the form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z=a+bi, \end{equation*}
    where \(a\) and \(b\) are real numbers, and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} i^2=-1. \end{equation*}
    The number \(a\) is the real part. The number \(b\) is the imaginary part.
    🔗
    We picture
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z=a+bi \end{equation*}
    as the point
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (a,b) \end{equation*}
    in the plane.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a+bi \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad (a,b). \end{equation*}
    The horizontal axis is called the real axis. The vertical axis is called the imaginary axis.
    🔗
    The distance from \(0\) to \(z\) is called the modulus of \(z\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} |z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}. \end{equation*}
    The angle \(\theta\) that \(z\) makes with the positive real axis is called an argument of \(z\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Thus
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a=r\cos\theta, \qquad b=r\sin\theta. \end{equation*}
    So
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z=a+bi = r\cos\theta+i r\sin\theta. \end{equation*}
    Factor out \(r\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta). \end{equation*}
    This is the polar form of a complex number.
    🔗

    Example 14.8.2. Example.

    Write
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z=1+i\sqrt3 \end{equation*}
    in polar form.
    🔗
    The modulus is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} r=\sqrt{1^2+(\sqrt3)^2}=\sqrt4=2. \end{equation*}
    The point \((1,\sqrt3)\) lies in Quadrant I, and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \tan\theta=\sqrt3. \end{equation*}
    Thus
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \theta=\frac{\pi}{3}. \end{equation*}
    So
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 1+i\sqrt3 = 2\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{3}+i\sin\frac{\pi}{3}\right). \end{equation*}
    As with polar coordinates, the angle is not unique. We can also write
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \theta=\frac{\pi}{3}+2\pi k \end{equation*}
    for any integer \(k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Multiplication as scaling and rotation

    The real advantage of polar form appears when we multiply.
    🔗
    Let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z_1=r_1(\cos\alpha+i\sin\alpha) \end{equation*}
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z_2=r_2(\cos\beta+i\sin\beta). \end{equation*}
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z_1z_2 = r_1r_2(\cos\alpha+i\sin\alpha)(\cos\beta+i\sin\beta). \end{equation*}
    Multiply the two parentheses:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (\cos\alpha\cos\beta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta) + i(\sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta). \end{equation*}
    By the angle addition formulas,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \cos\alpha\cos\beta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta=\cos(\alpha+\beta), \end{equation*}
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta=\sin(\alpha+\beta). \end{equation*}
    Therefore
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z_1z_2 = r_1r_2\bigl(\cos(\alpha+\beta)+i\sin(\alpha+\beta)\bigr). \end{equation*}
    This is the key rule.
    🔗

    Note 14.8.3. Multiplication in polar form.

    When complex numbers are multiplied:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{moduli multiply} \end{equation*}
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{arguments add}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    So multiplication by a complex number has two geometric effects:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{scale by its modulus} \end{equation*}
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{rotate by its argument}. \end{equation*}

    Example 14.8.4. multiplying by \(i\).

    The number \(i\) has polar form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} i=\cos\frac{\pi}{2}+i\sin\frac{\pi}{2}. \end{equation*}
    Its modulus is \(1\text{,}\) and its angle is \(\pi/2\text{.}\) Therefore multiplying by \(i\) rotates a point by \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise without changing its distance from the origin.
    🔗
    Algebraically,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} i(a+bi)=ai+b i^2=-b+ai. \end{equation*}
    So multiplication by \(i\) sends
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (a,b) \end{equation*}
    to
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (-b,a). \end{equation*}
    That is exactly a \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise rotation.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 14.8.6. multiplying two polar numbers.

    Let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z_1=2\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{6}+i\sin\frac{\pi}{6}\right) \end{equation*}
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z_2=3\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{4}+i\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\right). \end{equation*}
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z_1z_2 = 6\left(\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{6}+\frac{\pi}{4}\right) +i\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{6}+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\right). \end{equation*}
    Since
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\pi}{6}+\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{2\pi}{12}+\frac{3\pi}{12} = \frac{5\pi}{12}, \end{equation*}
    we get
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z_1z_2 = 6\left(\cos\frac{5\pi}{12}+i\sin\frac{5\pi}{12}\right). \end{equation*}
    The moduli multiplied:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 2\cdot3=6. \end{equation*}
    The angles added:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\pi}{6}+\frac{\pi}{4}=\frac{5\pi}{12}. \end{equation*}
    That is the whole multiplication.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Euler’s formula preview

    The expression
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \cos\theta+i\sin\theta \end{equation*}
    appears so often that it deserves a shorter name.
    🔗
    Later, power series will justify the remarkable identity
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta. \end{equation*}
    This is Euler’s formula.
    🔗
    For now, read it as a preview and a compact notation for a point on the unit circle.
    🔗
    With Euler’s formula, the polar form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) \end{equation*}
    becomes
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z=re^{i\theta}. \end{equation*}
    The multiplication rule becomes almost effortless:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (r_1e^{i\alpha})(r_2e^{i\beta}) = r_1r_2e^{i(\alpha+\beta)}. \end{equation*}
    So the usual exponential law
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} e^Ae^B=e^{A+B} \end{equation*}
    matches the geometry of rotations.
    🔗

    Note 14.8.7. Euler’s formula as geometry.

    The formula
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta \end{equation*}
    puts the unit circle into exponential notation. The angle \(\theta\) becomes the exponent.
    🔗
    🔗
    Some famous special cases fall out immediately.
    🔗
    When
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \theta=0, \end{equation*}
    we get
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} e^{0}=1. \end{equation*}
    When
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \theta=\frac{\pi}{2}, \end{equation*}
    we get
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} e^{i\pi/2}=i. \end{equation*}
    When
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \theta=\pi, \end{equation*}
    we get
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} e^{i\pi}=-1. \end{equation*}
    So
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} e^{i\pi}+1=0. \end{equation*}
    This compact equation connects five fundamental constants:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} e,\quad i,\quad \pi,\quad 1,\quad 0. \end{equation*}
    We will return to the proof after Taylor series, where \(e^x\text{,}\) \(\sin x\text{,}\) and \(\cos x\) will all be written as infinite polynomials.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Oscillation written exponentially

    Complex numbers also simplify oscillation.
    🔗
    A point moving around the unit circle at angular speed \(\omega\) has complex position
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z(t)=e^{i\omega t}. \end{equation*}
    Using Euler’s formula,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z(t)=\cos(\omega t)+i\sin(\omega t). \end{equation*}
    The real part is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \cos(\omega t), \end{equation*}
    and the imaginary part is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sin(\omega t). \end{equation*}
    So ordinary sine and cosine oscillations are shadows of circular motion.
    🔗
    The derivative is especially clean. Starting from
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z(t)=\cos(\omega t)+i\sin(\omega t), \end{equation*}
    differentiate:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z'(t) = -\omega\sin(\omega t)+i\omega\cos(\omega t). \end{equation*}
    Factor out \(i\omega\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} i\omega z(t) = i\omega\bigl(\cos(\omega t)+i\sin(\omega t)\bigr). \end{equation*}
    Since \(i^2=-1\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} i\omega z(t) = i\omega\cos(\omega t)-\omega\sin(\omega t). \end{equation*}
    This matches \(z’(t)\text{.}\) Therefore
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z'(t)=i\omega z(t). \end{equation*}
    Differentiating again gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z''(t)=i\omega z'(t)=i\omega(i\omega z(t)). \end{equation*}
    Since
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} i^2=-1, \end{equation*}
    we get
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z''(t)=-\omega^2 z(t). \end{equation*}
    Thus
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z''+\omega^2z=0. \end{equation*}
    Taking the real part gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x(t)=\cos(\omega t), \end{equation*}
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x''+\omega^2x=0. \end{equation*}
    Taking the imaginary part gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y(t)=\sin(\omega t), \end{equation*}
    and again
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y''+\omega^2y=0. \end{equation*}

    Note 14.8.9. Why complex exponentials are useful.

    The oscillation equation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x''+\omega^2x=0 \end{equation*}
    has sine and cosine solutions. Complex notation packages both into one function:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} e^{i\omega t}. \end{equation*}
    Differentiation becomes multiplication by \(i\omega\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    This is a bridge, not a new requirement for the rest of the chapter. But it shows where the story is going.
    🔗
    Parametric curves taught us to let time move a point in the plane. Polar coordinates taught us to measure that point by radius and angle. Complex numbers show that the same plane can be treated as a number system, where multiplication rotates.
    🔗
    The proof of Euler’s formula still waits. To prove it, we need a new kind of infinite process: not an infinite area, but an infinite sum of functions.
    🔗
