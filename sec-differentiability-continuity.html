    Section 4.5 Differentiability and continuity

    Differentiability requires that a curve locally resembles a straight line. Because lines cannot jump, any differentiable function must necessarily be continuous: differentiability is strictly stronger than continuity.
    🔗

    Subsection Differentiability implies continuity

    Theorem 4.5.1. Differentiability implies continuity.

    If \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\text{,}\) then \(f\) is continuous at \(a\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Since \(f'(a)\) exists as a finite real number, for \(h\neq 0\) we write:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f(a+h)-f(a) = h \cdot \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}. \end{equation*}
    Taking the limit as \(h\to 0\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{h\to 0}\bigl(f(a+h)-f(a)\bigr) = \lim_{h\to 0} h \cdot \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} = 0 \cdot f'(a) = 0. \end{equation*}
    Hence \(\lim_{h\to 0}f(a+h) = f(a)\text{,}\) proving that \(f\) is continuous at \(a\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Why the converse is false

    Continuity ensures that a curve does not break; differentiability demands a single limiting tangent slope. A curve can be connected without possessing a unique tangent slope.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Corners, cusps, vertical tangents, and jumps

    🔗

    Subsection The absolute value as the first warning example

    For \(f(x)=|x|\text{,}\) no single linear approximation \(|h| \approx mh\) can satisfy \(\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{|h|-mh}{h} = 0\text{,}\) because the left slope is \(-1\) and the right slope is \(+1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection A note about endpoints

    The standard derivative definition requires a two-sided limit. At boundary endpoints of intervals (e.g. \(x=0\) for \(\sqrt{x}\) on \([0,\infty)\)), only one-sided derivative limits can be formed.
