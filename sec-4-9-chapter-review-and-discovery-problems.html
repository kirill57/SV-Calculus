    Section 4.9 Chapter review and discovery problems

    The chapter began with a familiar quotient:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}. \end{equation*}
    That quotient measured average change. By shrinking the interval, we found a new number:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f'(a)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}. \end{equation*}
    The same number appeared in several forms. It was an instantaneous rate, a tangent slope, the coefficient in the best linear approximation, and velocity when the function was position.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \begin{array}{c|c} \amp \\ \hline \text{average rate of change} \amp \dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\\ \text{derivative at }a \amp f'(a)=\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}\\ \text{tangent line at }a \amp y-f(a)=f'(a)(x-a)\\ \text{local linear approximation} \amp f(a+h)\approx f(a)+f'(a)h\\ \text{differential approximation} \amp dy=f'(x)\,dx\\ \text{motion} \amp v(t)=s'(t),\qquad a(t)=v'(t)=s''(t) \end{array} \end{align*}
    The problems below are meant to make those meanings fluent. Some ask for calculations. Some ask for explanations. Some ask for judgment. Calculus needs all three.
    🔗

    Subsection Project: estimating velocity from noisy position data

    A motion sensor records position once every half-second. The object moves along a straight track. The sensor is good, but not perfect. Its readings contain small measurement errors.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \begin{array}{c|ccccccccc} t\text{ (seconds)} \amp 0 \amp 0.5 \amp 1.0 \amp 1.5 \amp 2.0 \amp 2.5 \amp 3.0 \amp 3.5 \amp 4.0\\ \hline s(t)\text{ (meters)} \amp 0.03 \amp 1.18 \amp 3.08 \amp 5.17 \amp 8.04 \amp 11.19 \amp 15.06 \amp 19.18 \amp 24.05 \end{array} \end{align*}
    The goal is to estimate the velocity at \(t=2\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Step 1: one-sided estimates.

    Use the forward difference
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{s(2.5)-s(2)}{0.5} \end{equation*}
    and the backward difference
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{s(2)-s(1.5)}{0.5}. \end{equation*}
    Compute both estimates. Are they equal? Should they be?
    🔗
    🔗

    Step 2: a centered estimate.

    Use the centered difference
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{s(2.5)-s(1.5)}{1.0}. \end{equation*}
    This uses points on both sides of \(t=2\text{.}\) Compare it with the two one-sided estimates.
    🔗
    🔗

    Step 3: a wider centered estimate.

    Use
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{s(3.0)-s(1.0)}{2.0}. \end{equation*}
    This uses data farther away from \(t=2\text{.}\) Compare this estimate with the centered estimate from Step 2.
    🔗
    🔗

    Step 4: repeat at other times.

    Estimate the velocity at \(t=1\) and \(t=3\) using centered differences.
    🔗
    For \(t=1\text{,}\) use
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{s(1.5)-s(0.5)}{1.0}. \end{equation*}
    For \(t=3\text{,}\) use
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{s(3.5)-s(2.5)}{1.0}. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Step 5: compare with a smooth model.

    The data were produced from a smooth motion close to
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} s_{\text{true}}(t)=t^2+2t, \end{equation*}
    with small measurement errors added.
    🔗
    For the smooth model,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} v_{\text{true}}(t)=s_{\text{true}}'(t)=2t+2. \end{equation*}
    Compute
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} v_{\text{true}}(1),\qquad v_{\text{true}}(2),\qquad v_{\text{true}}(3). \end{equation*}
    Compare these true velocities with your estimates from the noisy data.
    🔗
    🔗

    Step 6: the main question.

    A smaller time interval usually gives a better derivative estimate when the data are exact. But if the measurements are noisy, a very small interval can make the noise more visible because we divide by a small number.
    🔗
    Explain this tension in your own words:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{small interval captures local behavior,} \end{equation*}
    but
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{small interval can amplify measurement error.} \end{equation*}

    Subsection Hook: why the definition is not enough

    The derivative definition is the source of everything in this chapter:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}. \end{equation*}
    It tells us what the derivative means. It proves the first formulas. It catches corners, cusps, and jumps. It explains velocity, tangent slope, and local linear approximation.
    🔗
    But it is too slow to use from scratch every time.
    🔗
    For example, consider
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} F(x)=(x^2+1)(x^3-5). \end{equation*}
    With the tools of this chapter, we could expand first:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} F(x)=x^5+x^3-5x^2-5. \end{equation*}
    Then we can differentiate term by term:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} F'(x)=5x^4+3x^2-10x. \end{equation*}
    That works here because the product is a polynomial. But expansion is not always possible or pleasant. What about
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^2\sqrt{x+1}, \end{equation*}
    or
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x^3+1}{x^2-4}, \end{equation*}
    or, later,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sin(x^2)? \end{equation*}
    These functions are built from simpler pieces by multiplying, dividing, and composing. Their derivatives should be built from the derivatives of those pieces.
    🔗
    The next chapter turns the definition into a working language. The goal is not to replace meaning with rules. The goal is to make the meaning fast enough to use.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.9.5. Why is the definition the source, but not enough by itself?

    The limit definition tells what the derivative means and catches corners, yet it is too slow for everyday products, quotients, and compositions. In your own words, why must the next chapter keep the meaning while building a faster language?
    🔗
    Hint.
    Expanding a product into a polynomial is not always possible. The derivative of a built function should be built from the derivatives of the pieces.
